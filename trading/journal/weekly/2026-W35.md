## Lesson

**A pipeline that reports success when it fails is worse than one that reports nothing.**

The screener has failed **five** consecutive runs — 08-24 through 08-28 — every one of
them dying on `KeyError: 'market_cap'`, the first four after burning ~2.6 hours each. The
newest usable CSV is `screen_2026-08-21.csv`. Yet the git log reads:

```
6ca7348 chore(output): screener results 2026-08-27
7e0f79b chore(output): screener results 2026-08-26
b5e8de1 chore(output): screener results 2026-08-25
```

Four commits titled "screener results", containing no screener results — only the
`run_status.json` failure record. The history looks healthy from the outside. Nothing
alerts, nothing blocks, and the only way to find out is to open `run_status.json` and
read `"result": "failed"`.

The concrete cost: for four trading days the only legal source of new entries has been
a file whose every technical field (RSI, MACD, ADX, stochastics, %-from-high, price) is
priced off a stale close. That is not a small handicap — it is most of the edge. It
also directly caused this week's worst call, since the ORKA add was sized off
three-day-old technicals.

Two things follow. First, fixing `KeyError: 'market_cap'` is now higher-value than any
individual trade decision this account can make. Second, the daily session should read
`run_status.json` before the CSV and treat a failed run as a first-class input, not
something to notice incidentally from a file's mtime — and a failed run should not
produce a commit that claims results.

Secondary lesson, cheaper: broker price references can be wrong in ways that block
correct actions. Tonight Alpaca refused ORKA's $89.64 protective floor against an
internal reference of $87.935, while the actual close was $96.54 and after-hours
$96.55. Verifying against an independent source before acting on a broker price is now
a standing step, not a nicety.

## Friday 08-28 — what the Thursday draft could not know

Friday was the week, and it broke the Thursday draft's central assumption.

**Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's first Jackson Hole keynote put rate hikes back on the table.**
He said underlying inflation trends have not improved and the Fed "has more work to do";
the 2-year yield jumped 6bp+ to 4.298%, and a majority of investors moved to pricing a
September hike. The index absorbed it easily — S&P +0.72% to 7,730.99, Nasdaq +1.57%,
again on mega-cap AI — but **XBI fell more than 3%**.

That is the whole result. The market rose and the part of it this book is concentrated in
fell, for a structural reason rather than a random one: long-duration, pre-revenue equity
reprices directly off the discount rate. Inside the book, the sort order was almost exactly

## Amended lesson: two failures, and only one of them was the pipeline

The Thursday lesson — *a pipeline that reports success when it fails is worse than one
that reports nothing* — stands, and Friday's fifth failure and fifth "screener results"
commit reinforce it. But Friday added a second, more expensive one.

**A concentration you have named is still a concentration.** The Thursday draft identified
the healthcare/medtech sleeve at 35.8% of equity as "the main structural risk in this
book", wrote an explicit trim trigger for it, and then held. One day later a single macro
event took the whole sleeve down together, and the trigger did not fire — the sleeve fell
4.31%, short of the 8% threshold, and there was no fresh screener to test the rank
condition against. The trigger was well-intentioned and it was useless, because it was
written to detect deterioration in the *names* while the risk that actually arrived was in
the *environment they are priced in*.

Naming a risk, sizing it, and writing a trigger for it produced no protection whatsoever.
What would have helped is the thing I only worked out on Friday: sorting the sleeve by
**revenue vs. no revenue**, which is the same cut as duration, which is what a rate shock
prices. That test splits five names cleanly and it agreed exactly with which floors got
breached. It should have been applied when the concentration was first named, not after
the event that punished it.
