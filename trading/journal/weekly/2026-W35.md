## Lesson

**A pipeline that reports success when it fails is worse than one that reports nothing.**

The screener has failed four consecutive runs — 08-24, 08-25, 08-26, 08-27 — every one
of them dying on `KeyError: 'market_cap'` after burning ~2.6 hours. The newest usable
CSV is `screen_2026-08-21.csv`. Yet the git log reads:

```
6ca7348 chore(output): screener results 2026-08-27
7e0f79b chore(output): screener results 2026-08-26
b5e8de1 chore(output): screener results 2026-08-25
```

Four commits titled "screener results", containing no screener results — only the
`run_status.json` failure record. The history looks healthy from the outside. Nothing
alerts, nothing blocks, and the only way to find out is to open `run_status.json` and
read `"result": "failed"`.

The concrete cost: for four trading days the only legal source of new entries has been
a file whose every technical field (RSI, MACD, ADX, stochastics, %-from-high, price) is
priced off a stale close. That is not a small handicap — it is most of the edge. It
also directly caused this week's worst call, since the ORKA add was sized off
three-day-old technicals.

Two things follow. First, fixing `KeyError: 'market_cap'` is now higher-value than any
individual trade decision this account can make. Second, the daily session should read
`run_status.json` before the CSV and treat a failed run as a first-class input, not
something to notice incidentally from a file's mtime — and a failed run should not
produce a commit that claims results.

Secondary lesson, cheaper: broker price references can be wrong in ways that block
correct actions. Tonight Alpaca refused ORKA's $89.64 protective floor against an
internal reference of $87.935, while the actual close was $96.54 and after-hours
$96.55. Verifying against an independent source before acting on a broker price is now
a standing step, not a nicety.
